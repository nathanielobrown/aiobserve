# Writing style guide

How to write effectively; based on William Zinsser's *On Writing Well*.

## What good writing is

Good writing is a personal transaction between one writer and one reader. Its two essential qualities are humanity and warmth: a person visible behind the words, saying plainly what they mean. Clutter is the enemy: every word that serves no function, every long word that could be a short word, every adverb that repeats its verb, every passive construction that hides who is doing what. Clear writing is clear thinking; if the reader is lost, the writer wasn't careful enough.

## Before you write

Settle the unities, inferring them from the task and the material (state your assumptions in one line if they matter):

- **Audience and capacity**: who is the one reader, and who are you to them?
- **Pronoun, tense, tone**: pick each and hold it. Never drift from plain narration into brochure-speak.
- **Scope**: think small. Decide what corner of the subject to bite off, cover it well, and stop.
- **The point**: an essay or announcement leaves the reader with one provocative thought — not two, or five. A technical document may have to carry many points; give it a spine instead. Decide what the reader should know or be able to do when they finish, make each section earn one point toward that, and cut whatever serves none of them.

## The rules

**Simplicity.** Strip every sentence to its cleanest components. Most first drafts can be cut by 50 percent without losing information or voice; assume yours is no exception. Ask of every word: is it doing new work? If you're not sure, it isn't. Prefer the short word: "help" not "assistance," "many" not "numerous," "try" not "attempt," "now" not "at this point in time," "because" not "due to the fact that." Cut throat-clearing ("It should be pointed out," "It is interesting to note") and prepositions draped on verbs ("free up," "head up," "face up to").

**Verbs.** Active and precise. "Joe saw him," not "he was seen by Joe." Don't say the president "stepped down" — did he resign, retire, or get fired? Prefer short Anglo-Saxon words to long Latin ones.

**Adverbs and adjectives.** Most are unnecessary. The radio didn't blare loudly; "blare" is loud. No tall skyscrapers or yellow daffodils. Keep only modifiers that do work the noun or verb can't: "He looked at the gray sky and the black clouds and decided to sail back to the harbor."

**Qualifiers.** Prune "a bit," "sort of," "rather," "quite," "very," "pretty much," "somewhat," "arguably." Every little qualifier whittles away some fraction of the reader's trust. Don't be kind of bold. Be bold.

**People, not concepts.** No sentences where the meaning sits in abstract nouns ("The common reaction is incredulous laughter"). Get people doing things ("Most people just laugh with disbelief"). Never string nouns into a chain ("communication facilitation skills development intervention").

**Voice.** Be a person, not an institution. Only write what you would comfortably say aloud — no "indeed," "moreover," "utilize," "individual" for a person. Use contractions ("don't," "I'll"; avoid the ambiguous "I'd"). Use "I" where allowed; otherwise think "I" and write with that warmth. Write to please yourself, not to placate every imaginable reader: humor and opinion are allowed, and if a line amuses you, keep it. Don't hedge. Zinsser's champion wishy-washy sentence — "And yet, on balance, affirmative action has, I think, been a qualified success" — packs five hedges into 13 words. Say what you mean and stand behind it.

**Include everyone.** Go plural, use "we" or "you," or choose a generic noun ("families," not "wives and children") instead of constructions that fence readers out. "You" is especially warm in instructional writing.

**Jargon and journalese.** No "famed," "upcoming," "notables," "firing off a note." No corporate euphemism: layoffs are layoffs, not "involuntary methodologies." New words must fill a real need ("dropout" yes, "learnings" no — say "lessons"). Offer ideas, not "input"; keep classic distinctions (fewer/less, infer/imply). But in technical writing, precision outranks plainness where they conflict: keep the exact technical term and put plain words around it.

**Sound.** Readers hear prose in their inner ear. Vary sentence length; an occasional short sentence carries a tremendous punch. "These are the times that try men's souls" is poetry; "Soulwise, these are trying times" is oatmeal. Reread your draft as if aloud and fix what plods.

**Flow.** Signal every shift of direction or time as early in the sentence as possible: "but," "yet," "still," "instead," "meanwhile," "now," "later." Start sentences with "but" freely; never start or end with "however" — tuck it in early. After each sentence, ask where you left the reader. Watch the joints: in "The tragic hero is Othello. Small and malevolent, Iago feeds his jealous suspicions," the name in the reader's ear is Othello.

**Punctuation.** Reach the period sooner; a mired sentence is two sentences. Use the dash to amplify ("We decided to keep going — it was only 100 miles more") or to tuck in a side thought. Semicolons sparingly, colons for lists, exclamation points almost never — don't notify the reader of a joke; let them find it. Use "that" unless it makes the meaning ambiguous; "which," set off by a comma, identifies or explains.

**Truth and restraint.** Don't overstate; the living room did not look as if an atomic bomb had gone off. One bogus claim makes everything after it suspect. Trust your material: a self-sufficient fact needs no "surprisingly," "predictably" or "of course" — give readers room to do their own marveling.

**Leads.** The first sentence is the most important; if it doesn't pull the reader to the second, the piece is dead. An essay opens with freshness, surprise, a story, or a specific odd fact. A technical document opens plainer but under the same law: say immediately what this is, who it's for, and what they'll be able to do — no history lessons, no stock openers (the visitor from Mars, "What do X, Y and Z have in common?"). Every paragraph amplifies the last; the final sentence of each paragraph is the springboard to the next.

**Endings.** When you're ready to stop, stop — no "In sum" or "In conclusion" recap of what the reader just read. An essay earns an ending that surprises yet seems exactly right: a fact with finality, a quotation, an echo of the opening. A technical document simply ends when the information is delivered.

**Structure.** Short paragraphs with air around them — but not a chatter of one-line fragments. Structure is the road map of your thinking; let it carry the multiple points so no single paragraph has to.

## Technical documents

Most of what we write is technical — docs, READMEs, design notes, PR descriptions. The rules above govern every sentence; these govern the page.

**Headings.** A reader skimming only the headings should come away with the document's spine, so make each one say something: "Rotate keys quarterly," not "Key management."

**Bullets.** A list is for enumerable facts: items the reader will scan, count, or check off, in any order. The moment one bullet leans on the bullet before it, the list is a paragraph in disguise — write the paragraph. Keep nesting shallow; by the third level you've built an outline, not a document.

**Show the thing.** A code snippet beats a description of code, a diagram beats a paragraph of architecture, a table beats comparative prose. When a fact has a natural shape — a command, a payload, a flow — present it in that shape, and spend your sentences on what the artifact can't say: why it's built this way, what to watch for. Prefer real examples to invented ones.

## Rewriting

Rewriting is the essence of writing well. Draft, then rewrite: reshape and tighten. Cut whole thoughts, not just words: the reason that restates the last, the guard against a reader you don't have. Drop the points the reader doesn't need — they bury the ones they do.

Run the bracket test on every sentence: is every word doing new work? Can any thought be expressed with more economy? Is anything pompous, pretentious or faddish? Am I keeping something useless because I think it's beautiful? When a phrase resists every repair, ask whether you need it at all. Usually not — delete it and watch the sentence spring to life.

## Rewriting someone else's draft

Facts come first, whatever the draft's origin:

- Preserve facts and invent none. If a claim is unclear or looks wrong, flag it rather than paper over it
- Rewriting can launder a shaky claim into confident prose. When you can't verify a claim the piece depends on, keep the flag visible in your notes instead of polishing doubt away

Match your approach to who wrote the draft:

- **A human wrote it**: preserve the author's voice — prune the clutter, don't transplant a toupee. The goal is the writer at their clearest, not you
- **A machine wrote it** (AI output, boilerplate, committee prose): there is no voice to preserve, so don't amplify its tics. Strip the tells: hedges on every claim, throat-clearing ("It's important to note"), inflated adjectives ("robust," "comprehensive," "seamless"), the reflexive rule of three, empty antithesis ("not only X but Y," "it's not X — it's Y"; if cutting the negative half loses nothing, it was decoration), prose squeezed into telegraphic fragments, and a conclusion that restates the piece. Rewrite freely, in the plain voice of a person who knows the material and means what they say

Either way, keep the document's required conventions (format, headings, links) unless asked to change them.
